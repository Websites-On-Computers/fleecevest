# Ruthless-Clarity PM Communication Reference

> distilled: 2026-08-20, corpus @ be8ab89

## Sales, Then Logistics — Wes Kao
Never open with process details; spend the first 30 seconds re-selling why the thing matters, because buy-in decays even after people "already agreed." **When to use:** Any ask in Slack, email, or a meeting — especially requests to people who signed off weeks ago. *Source: Wes Kao, "Persuasive communication and managing up | Wes Kao (Maven, altMBA, Section4)"*
> "a sales note is different than a logistics note"

## Most Obvious Objection (MOO) — Wes Kao
Before sending anything, spend a few seconds naming the most obvious pushback and pre-address it in the message itself — the objection you skip is the one that derails the thread. **When to use:** Right before any pitch, strategy doc, or ask; if you can't answer the MOO, your plan has a gap. *Source: Wes Kao, "Persuasive communication and managing up | Wes Kao (Maven, altMBA, Section4)"*
> "MOO stands for Most Obvious Objection."

## Know the Lead Before You Unbury It — Wes Kao
Concision is density, not brevity — a rambling message is usually a symptom that you haven't figured out your own core point yet, so edit for the point, not the word count. **When to use:** When a draft feels long or buried; stop cutting words and first write the one-sentence lead. *Source: Wes Kao, "Persuasive communication and managing up | Wes Kao (Maven, altMBA, Section4)"*
> "you can't cut to the chase unless you know what the chase is"

## Feedback Is Strategy, Not Self-Expression — Wes Kao
Cut everything from critical feedback that exists to vent your frustration; keep only the ~10% that will actually change the person's behavior. **When to use:** Delivering hard feedback after you've been silently annoyed for a while — that's when venting masquerades as candor. *Source: Wes Kao, "Persuasive communication and managing up | Wes Kao (Maven, altMBA, Section4)"*
> "trim everything else that you were about to say that does not actually contribute to that goal and only keep the part that will make the person want to change"

## What / So What / Now What — Matt Abrahams
Structure any spontaneous update in three moves: what it is, why it matters, what happens next — answer-first with the action attached. **When to use:** Standups, status pings, and explaining a decision on the spot without a doc. *Source: Matt Abrahams, "How to speak more confidently and persuasively | Matt Abrahams (professor, speaker, author)"*
> "here's what the feature is, here's why it's important, and here's what we're doing about it"

## PREP — Matt Abrahams
When asked for an opinion cold, answer as Point, Reason, Example, then restate the Point — you sound decisive because you led with the answer. **When to use:** Getting put on the spot by an exec ("what do you think of this?") with zero prep time. *Source: Matt Abrahams, "How to speak more confidently and persuasively | Matt Abrahams (professor, speaker, author)"*
> "make your point, give a reason for making that point, give an example"

## Dare to Be Dull — Matt Abrahams
Under pressure, deliberately lower the bar from "best possible answer" to "just answer the question" — the bandwidth self-judgment consumes is what was making you incoherent. **When to use:** Q&A, exec grillings, any high-stakes on-the-spot moment when nerves spike. *Source: Matt Abrahams, "How to speak more confidently and persuasively | Matt Abrahams (professor, speaker, author)"*
> "connection over perfection by daring to be dull"

## Title-as-Takeaway Slides — Andy Raskin
Every slide title should state the slide's conclusion ("Retention doubled after onboarding rework"), never a label ("Retention data") — the exec should extract the point with zero work. **When to use:** Any exec deck or review doc; if a title could sit atop a slide arguing the opposite, rewrite it. *Source: Andy Raskin, "The power of strategic narrative | Andy Raskin"*
> "the title the takeaway of the slide so that the person looking at it has to do zero work to take away"

## Old Game / New Game — Andy Raskin
Don't pitch problem-then-solution like an "arrogant doctor"; open by naming a shift in the world that makes the old way a losing game, so your proposal becomes a movement to join rather than a product to evaluate. **When to use:** Strategy pitches, positioning docs, and any narrative meant to align a cross-functional org. *Source: Andy Raskin, "The power of strategic narrative | Andy Raskin"*
> "Every movie starts with some kind of shift in the world, and I call this shift the shift from the old game to a new game."

## Ship the Shit Draft — Andy Raskin
A deliberately rough first draft that the team can tear apart beats trying to synthesize everyone's input into a perfect narrative — reaction is what produces the sharp version. **When to use:** Aligning leadership on any high-stakes doc; short-circuits design-by-committee. *Source: Andy Raskin, "The power of strategic narrative | Andy Raskin"*
> "shit draft is a million times more valuable than having all these great ideas"

## What Is / What Could Be / New Bliss — Nancy Duarte
Persuade by oscillating between current reality and the possible future several times, then land on the "new bliss" — the world once your idea is adopted; the contrast creates the pull. **When to use:** Any moment of influence, from a funding ask to a hallway pitch — not just keynotes. *Source: Nancy Duarte, "Storytelling with Nancy Duarte: How to craft compelling presentations and tell a story that sticks"*
> "what is, what could be, what is, what could be? And then at the last what could be you state the last horizontal line is what we call the new bliss."

## Start With Location + Action — Matthew Dicks
Open every story with where you are and something already happening — no throat-clearing, no backstory — because it signals "the movie's on" and buys you the room's attention. **When to use:** Opening any anecdote in a pitch, all-hands, or customer story; kill the preamble. *Source: Matthew Dicks, "How to tell better stories | Matthew Dicks (Storyworthy)"*
> "start every story you ever tell for the rest of your life with those two things, and you're already going to be like 50% better"

## The Five-Second Moment — Matthew Dicks
A story is about one brief moment of change; identify that moment first and build everything else backward as context for it — if you can't name the moment, you don't have a story yet. **When to use:** Before telling any customer anecdote or founding story; find the moment, then trim to what serves it. *Source: Matthew Dicks, "How to tell better stories | Matthew Dicks (Storyworthy)"*
> "98% of the story is the context to bring that singular moment into fruition"

## Ruinous Empathy — Kim Scott
Most feedback failure isn't cruelty, it's "kindness": caring personally without challenging directly, which quietly costs people promotions and eventually their jobs. **When to use:** Any time you're tempted to soften or delay bad news to spare someone's feelings — that's the failure mode, not the fix. *Source: Kim Scott, "Radical Candor episode (corpus title metadata mislabeled)"*
> "90% of us make 90% of our mistakes in that ruinous empathy bucket"

## CORE: Context, Observation, Result, Next Step — Kim Scott
Deliver criticism (and praise) as a four-beat sequence about the behavior and its consequence, never the person's personality — it makes hard news specific and actionable in under a minute. **When to use:** Any impromptu feedback moment, praise or criticism, especially right after the event. *Source: Kim Scott, "Radical Candor episode (corpus title metadata mislabeled)"*
> "for both praise and criticism, you want to use sort of context, observation, result, next step"

## The Go-To Question Plus Six Seconds — Kim Scott
"Do you have any feedback for me?" always gets "no"; ask one specific, personal question instead, then endure the silence until they answer for real. **When to use:** Weekly 1:1s with reports, peers, and your own boss — earn the right to give candor by soliciting it first. *Source: Kim Scott, "Radical Candor episode (corpus title metadata mislabeled)"*
> "The question that I like to ask is, 'What could I do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me?'"

## Suspend Judgment on Your Critical Path — Jeffrey Pfeffer
Whether you like or respect someone whose cooperation you need is irrelevant — and your disdain will leak; the only judgment that matters is that they're on your critical path. **When to use:** Cross-functional work with someone you find difficult or incompetent; influence dies the moment they sense your contempt. *Source: Jeffrey Pfeffer, "The paths to power: How to grow your influence and advance your career | Jeffrey Pfeffer (Stanford)"*
> "if somebody is on your critical path, the only judgment you should make is they're on my critical path"

## Broker Power via Weak Ties — Jeffrey Pfeffer
Influence without authority comes from being the connector: deliberately cultivate weak ties outside your circle so you're the person who can match people, ideas, and resources. **When to use:** Building coalition capacity before you need it — a PM's power base is a network, not a title. *Source: Jeffrey Pfeffer, "The paths to power: How to grow your influence and advance your career | Jeffrey Pfeffer (Stanford)"*
> "networking is a fabulous way to get knowledge about people and about ideas"

## Looks Beat Content — Jeffrey Pfeffer
Audiences respond first to how you look, second to how you sound, and least to what you say — posture, eye contact, and brevity read as competence independent of substance. **When to use:** High-stakes presentations to execs or boards; rehearse the delivery, not just the slides. *Source: Jeffrey Pfeffer, "The paths to power: How to grow your influence and advance your career | Jeffrey Pfeffer (Stanford)"*
> "how people look, secondarily to how they sound, and by far the least important to the content of what they say"

## Decompose Disagreement Into Hypotheses — Julie Zhuo
When you think a directive from above is wrong, don't argue good-vs-bad; break it into its underlying assumptions, name the one you doubt, and propose the smallest test of just that assumption. **When to use:** Pushing back on an exec decision without torching the relationship — convert opposition into a counter-proposal. *Source: Julie Zhuo, "From managing people to managing AI: The leadership skills everyone needs now | Julie Zhuo"*
> "You can probably decompose it from a blanket it's good or bad to like, 'Okay, this is a hypothesis, this is a hypothesis, this is a hypothesis.'"
