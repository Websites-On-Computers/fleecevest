# Discovery & Evidence Playbook

> distilled: 2026-08-20, corpus @ be8ab89

## Forces of Progress — Bob Moesta
A switch happens only when push (situation) + pull (new outcome) outweigh anxiety (of the new) + habit (of the present) — features alone don't explain purchase decisions. **When to use:** Framing win/loss or churn interviews around causality instead of a feature checklist. *Source: Bob Moesta, "How to find work you love"*
> "if F1 and F2 are not greater than F3 and F4, they're not going to move."

## "Bitchin' ain't switchin'" — Bob Moesta
Loud complaints and stated intent to leave aren't predictive of actual switching behavior — only interview people who already acted. **When to use:** A vocal feature request is driving roadmap pressure and you need to check if it's real signal. *Source: Bob Moesta, "How to find work you love"*
> "Just because people bitch about something doesn't mean they're going to do anything about it."

## Layers of Language — Bob Moesta
Get past rehearsed answers by never asking "why" repeatedly — ask for examples, and deliberately play back a wrong summary so the subject corrects and elaborates. **When to use:** Live customer interviews where answers feel vague or overly polished. *Source: Bob Moesta, "How to find work you love"*
> "I play things back incorrectly because they're going to say no, and I'm going to say, 'All right, fix it,' and then they'll talk more. The moment somebody says yes, there's nothing more to say."

## 3–6 Month Recency Window — Gia Laudi
Recruit interview/survey subjects who converted 3–6 months ago: recent enough to remember "life before," seasoned enough to have real usage. **When to use:** Screening candidates for a discovery research round. *Source: Georgiana Laudi, "Customer-led growth"*
> "if you go to somebody that's been your customer for two years, they're just going to fill answers with what they think might have been going on in their life."

## Survey-First, Escalate to Interviews Only If Needed — Gia Laudi
Run a structured survey before booking interviews; if the results are decisive, skip the interviews entirely rather than treating 1:1s as mandatory. **When to use:** Discovery is at risk of stalling into analysis paralysis and speed matters. *Source: Georgiana Laudi, "Customer-led growth"*
> "We didn't end up actually needing to run the interviews because the surveys that we ran were pretty decisive and clear in terms of what we learned."

## Skipping Discovery Kills Launches — Krithika Shankarraman
Stripe's Relay ("buy button") shipped with fanfare and failed because the team hadn't validated real demand or adoption dynamics before building. **When to use:** A new platform bet depends on multiple parties adopting something net-new — pressure-test demand before committing engineering. *Source: Krithika Shankarraman, "Growth tactics from OpenAI and Stripe's first marketer"*
> "we hadn't gone deep enough into the market dynamics. We hadn't done enough user research. Did people really want this?"

## The Nielsen Number — Shaun Clowes
Qualitative learning saturates between 7 and 14 interviews — fewer and you're under-informed, more and you're burning cycles for no new insight. **When to use:** Scoping how many customers to interview for a discovery sprint. *Source: Shaun Clowes, "Why great AI products are all about the data"*
> "once you interview between 7 and 14 people, you stop learning new things."

## Leading Questions Poison the Well — Shaun Clowes
Most PMs sabotage their own research by asking questions engineered to confirm what they already believe. **When to use:** Reviewing an interview script before running it — check every question for a presupposed answer. *Source: Shaun Clowes, "Why great AI products are all about the data"*
> "they go into these conversations asking leading questions, which really are designed to get the customer to say what they already want to be true."

## Click Left, Click Right, Click Up — Shaun Clowes
Before acting on a striking data point or piece of feedback, check what happened immediately before and after it, then zoom out to whether it still matters to the real goal. **When to use:** A compelling signal shows up and someone wants to act on it immediately — this is the gut-check before treating it as evidence. *Source: Shaun Clowes, "Why great AI products are all about the data"*
> "if you go a click to the left, a click to the right... and then a click up and you still see the thing that tells you the story that you want to tell, then now you've got something that's very compelling."

## Diagnose the Problem, Don't Accept the Proposed Fix — Bob Baxley
When feedback arrives as a literal solution ("make it blue"), treat it as a symptom, not a spec — dig for the underlying problem and let the team generate the real fix. **When to use:** Turning stakeholder or user feedback into evidence instead of a backlog of literal asks. *Source: Bob Baxley, "35 years of product design wisdom from Apple, Disney, Pinterest and beyond"*
> "'Oh, they couldn't see it, so it wasn't prominent enough, right?' ... 'There's a 100 different things we could do to make it more prominent, one of which is making it blue.'"

## Fidelity Poisons Feedback — Bob Baxley
High-resolution mockups pull feedback toward colors and shapes instead of usability and value — stay low-fidelity to keep the conversation conceptual. **When to use:** Deciding what fidelity to show stakeholders or users when you want feedback on the concept, not the paint job. *Source: Bob Baxley, "35 years of product design wisdom from Apple, Disney, Pinterest and beyond"*
> "As soon as you produce something in high resolution the feedback you're going to get is going to be about colors and shapes... very loosely related to usability and value."

## Twyman's Law — Ronny Kohavi
Any experiment result that looks unusually large or surprising is more likely a measurement error than a real effect. **When to use:** An A/B test shows a dramatic win before you celebrate or ship it. *Source: Ronny Kohavi, "The ultimate guide to A/B testing"*
> "if the result looks too good to be true, your normal movement of an experiment is under 1% and you suddenly have a 10% movement, hold the celebratory dinner... investigate."

## Sample Ratio Mismatch Check — Ronny Kohavi
If the actual traffic split deviates from the designed ratio by more than chance allows, the entire experiment result is untrustworthy regardless of what the metrics say. **When to use:** As a mandatory automated gate before anyone is allowed to read an experiment scorecard. *Source: Ronny Kohavi, "The ultimate guide to A/B testing"*
> "If you get something off from 50%, it's a red flag."

## Hierarchy of Evidence — Ronny Kohavi
Trust should scale with evidence type: anecdote is weakest, observational studies next, controlled and replicated experiments strongest. **When to use:** Deciding how much weight to give any claim — internal or external — before acting on it. *Source: Ronny Kohavi, "The ultimate guide to A/B testing"*
> "If something is anecdotal, don't trust it... As you get more up and up to a natural experiment, and controlled experiments, and multiple controlled experiments, your trust levels should go up."

## DHM Framework — Gibson Biddle
Evaluate any product bet against three tests at once — does it Delight customers, is it Hard-to-copy, is it Margin-enhancing — not customer delight alone. **When to use:** Framing or stress-testing a product strategy bet before investing. *Source: Gibson Biddle, Lenny's Podcast (Netflix strategy episode; title field is mislabeled in the source transcript)*
> "I mainly was just trying to find these high level theories and hypotheses that I hoped would delight in hard to copy, margin enhancing ways."

## MVP Tests a Hypothesis, Not "Ship Something Crappy" — Eric Ries
MVP quality is defined entirely by what's needed to validate a specific leap-of-faith hypothesis, not by craft standards or a bias toward sloppiness. **When to use:** A team is either over-building out of fear or shipping sloppily for no reason tied to the actual test. *Source: Eric Ries, "Reflections on a movement"*
> "quality is defined by the customer. And if we don't know who our customer is, we literally don't know what the word quality means."

## Leap-of-Faith Assumptions First — Eric Ries
Before scoping an MVP, brainstorm and rank the leap-of-faith assumptions behind the plan, then design the smallest test for the riskiest one. **When to use:** A team can't agree on MVP scope because it hasn't agreed on what belief is actually being tested. *Source: Eric Ries, "Reflections on a movement"*
> "you should first brainstorm your leap of faith assumptions and categorize them this way and select them like this... now we've got the leap of faith assumption. Let's go find out."
