# Operating Habits: How the Best PMs Actually Work

> distilled: 2026-08-20, corpus @ be8ab89

## LNO Framework — Shreyas Doshi
Classify every task as Leverage (10-100x return), Neutral (~1x), or Overhead (negative return) before deciding how much polish it deserves — the same kind of task can be any of the three depending on context. **When to use:** Before deciding how much time to sink into anything, from a bug report to a launch deck. *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*
> "The L tasks are such that when you put in a certain amount of effort, you get 10X or 100X in return in terms of impact."

## Minimize Opportunity Cost, Not ROI — Shreyas Doshi
In a high-leverage role almost everything clears "positive ROI," so that filter just rewards quick, low-effort wins — ask instead what the single best use of the time would have been. **When to use:** Quarterly planning, when the roadmap is full of safe wins and quietly avoiding the one ambiguous bet that could change the business. *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*
> "You should stop doing work that simply provides a positive return on investment... and start focusing on work that minimizes opportunity cost."

## Tigers, Paper Tigers, and Elephants — Shreyas Doshi
Before a launch, have the team silently write down what could kill the project in six months, then sort each risk into a real threat (tiger), an overblown fear (paper tiger), or the thing everyone privately knows but won't say (elephant) — then vote and assign owners. **When to use:** Before any launch big enough that a failure would trigger a post-mortem — run this instead. *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*
> "If you do a pre-mortem right, you will not have to do an ugly post-mortem."

## Impact / Execution / Optics — Shreyas Doshi
People arguing past each other in a review are often just defaulting to different levels — execs default to impact, PMs mid-launch default to execution, some orgs over-reward optics until it becomes the actual goal. **When to use:** When a debate feels like two smart people talking past each other — name the level each side is arguing from instead of re-litigating the surface disagreement. *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*

## Creative vs. Reactive Leadership — Ken Norton
Leaders default into one of three reactive postures — complying (need to be liked), protecting (need to be right), controlling (need to win) — instead of acting from purpose; most leaders operate reactively most of the time. **When to use:** As a real-time check mid-decision — if you're optimizing for being liked, right, or in control, name it and step back out. *Source: Ken Norton, "How to unlock your product leadership skills"*
> "Some 75% of leaders are primarily operating reactively."

## Protect Bandwidth for 10X, Not Just 10% — Ken Norton
Teams default to a portfolio of safe incremental bets unless a leader explicitly carves out capacity for high-risk, high-payoff swings — the idea usually already exists, the venue for it to survive doesn't. **When to use:** Allocating a planning cycle — reserve a slice for moonshot work instead of letting the roadmap fill with guaranteed wins. *Source: Ken Norton, "How to unlock your product leadership skills"*
> "Kodak invented the digital camera... There just wasn't an environment created where the people who had that idea... could step up to the plate and try."

## "How Did the Bill Become a Law?" — Ken Norton
Skip the scripted, rehearsable interview questions and instead ask a candidate to walk through how something they recently shipped actually came to be — it reveals real working style far better than a prepared answer. **When to use:** Running a PM interview loop, or as a question a candidate should ask an employer back. *Source: Ken Norton, "How to unlock your product leadership skills"*
> "Ask them to pick an example of something they've shipped recently and just talk about how it came to be. How did the bill become a law?"

## Diagnose with Data, Treat with Design — Julie Zhuo
Data can tell you whether you have a problem and roughly where it lives — it can't tell you the fix; the fix still requires a creative, undata-able leap. **When to use:** Whenever a team is deciding how much weight to give an A/B test versus a design judgment call. *Source: Julie Zhuo, "From managing people to managing AI: The leadership skills everyone needs now"*
> "What you really want is you want to diagnose with data and treat with design."

## Decompose Disagreement into Assumptions — Julie Zhuo
When you're asked to execute something you privately doubt, don't argue the conclusion — break it into the underlying hypotheses, isolate the one you actually doubt, and turn just that into a small, specific test. **When to use:** Managing up on a directive you're skeptical of, without rubber-stamping it or staging an open revolt. *Source: Julie Zhuo, "From managing people to managing AI: The leadership skills everyone needs now"*
> "The reason I didn't like this proposal is because I believe that this assumption is wrong... can we get some data?"

## The Three-Part Strategy Test — Jackie Bavaro
A real strategy needs a vision, a strategic framework (market, definition of winning, the big bets), and a roadmap used not as a commitment but as a backward gut-check on whether the vision is even reachable at the current pace. **When to use:** Whenever someone hands you a number ("grow revenue 50%") and calls it a strategy. *Source: Jackie Bavaro, "Jackie Bavaro on getting better at product strategy"*
> "A roadmap in strategy is not a commitment. Instead, it's a way to double check if your plan makes any sense at all."

## Six-Month Strategy Moratorium — Jackie Bavaro
A PM new to a team shouldn't try to rewrite the strategy in the first six months — spend that window doing customer research and executing what exists, then pitch your own once the pattern-recognition has accumulated. **When to use:** Onboarding into a new product, and as the standard to hold new hires to. *Source: Jackie Bavaro, "Jackie Bavaro on getting better at product strategy"*
> "I think that for your first six months on a product, probably don't worry about strategy... I'm going to deliver on that strategy."

## Say Yes for Two Weeks — Jackie Bavaro
Early-career PMs default to "say no efficiently" to protect scope; a forced two-week experiment of saying yes to everything showed that agreeing a problem is real is different from committing to the requester's specific solution. **When to use:** When you notice you've slipped into reflexive gatekeeper mode instead of mining requests for the real problem. *Source: Jackie Bavaro, "Jackie Bavaro on getting better at product strategy"*

## Traction, Not Distraction — Nir Eyal
The real divide isn't distraction vs. focus, it's distraction vs. traction — anything done with intent, even leisure, counts as traction; anything that pulls you off what you planned, even "productive" email-checking, is distraction. **When to use:** The moment you can't tell if what you're doing counts as work — check it against your calendar, not against how virtuous it feels. *Source: Nir Eyal, "Strategies for becoming less distractible and improving focus"*
> "You can't call something a distraction unless you know what it distracted you from."

## The Calendar Is the Only Real To-Do List — Nir Eyal
Weekly, block actual calendar time against your values across self, relationships, and work, including leisure — to-do lists fail because they're unconstrained and let you feel busy while dodging what's hard. **When to use:** Replacing daily to-do-list planning, and for managing up by showing the calendar instead of just saying no. *Source: Nir Eyal, "Strategies for becoming less distractible and improving focus"*
> "You don't tell your boss no, you ask your boss to help you prioritize."

## The Tobi Tornado — Tobi Lütke
The moment he suspects a project is off track, he forces an immediate, direct conversation instead of letting it drift — he's either wrong and needs to update fast, or right and it's unfair to let people keep working on something doomed. **When to use:** When leadership senses a project heading nowhere — compress the reckoning into one direct conversation instead of running out the sunk cost. *Source: Tobi Lütke, "Tobi Lütke's leadership playbook: First principles, infinite games, and maximizing human potential"*
> "Once I imagine something might be not the right thing to work on I'm either incorrect... or I'm correct at which point it's super unfair for letting people work on something that isn't going to make it."

## Taste Over Metrics — Tobi Lütke
Shopify's core product org deliberately skips OKRs in favor of taste, quality, and signals like fun and pride, because any metric that becomes a goal stops being a good one — data informs, humans still decide. **When to use:** Designing how a product org sets goals, especially resisting the pull to make everything measurable just because it's easier to manage. *Source: Tobi Lütke, "Tobi Lütke's leadership playbook: First principles, infinite games, and maximizing human potential"*
> "Goodhart's law just says, 'Any metric that becomes a goal ceases to be a good metric.'"

## Run Toward the Decision — Ben Horowitz
Real leadership calls are almost never good-vs-bad, they're bad-vs-worse; hesitating because every option looks ugly is the most destructive failure mode because it signals dysfunction and invites a power vacuum. **When to use:** Whenever you catch yourself avoiding a call because every visible option looks bad. *Source: Ben Horowitz, "$46B of hard truths: Why founders fail and why you need to run toward fear"*
> "The only value you ever add is when you make a decision that most people don't like."

## The Managerial Leverage Test — Ben Horowitz
A report has leverage when they're telling you what the department should do next, not the other way around; if you're the one generating the ideas and pushing them, you have zero leverage and might as well run the team yourself. **When to use:** Deciding whether someone has grown into a role, or whether it's time to make a change. *Source: Ben Horowitz, "$46B of hard truths: Why founders fail and why you need to run toward fear"*
> "If I have the ideas about what your department should do next... that's no leverage. What's leverage is if you're telling me what you should do."

## Sales, Then Logistics — Wes Kao
Never open an ask with the how — deadline, format, process — before establishing the why; logistics-first reads as just another task and gets buried instead of acted on, even with people who already agreed in principle. **When to use:** Any message asking someone to do something, even a routine ask to someone already bought in. *Source: Wes Kao, "Persuasive communication and managing up"*
> "A sales note is meant to get people excited to do the thing you want them to do... only then, after they have bought in, does it make sense to share the logistics."

## MOO: Most Obvious Objection — Wes Kao
Before saying or writing anything, spend a few seconds asking what the most obvious objection to it would be, and pre-address it — treated as a constant reflex, not a one-time step reserved for big presentations. **When to use:** Before any pitch, message, or recommendation where pushback is likely. *Source: Wes Kao, "Persuasive communication and managing up"*
> "MOO stands for Most Obvious Objection."
