# Prioritization, OKRs, and Decision Quality

> distilled: 2026-08-20, corpus @ be8ab89

## LNO framework — Shreyas Doshi
Every task is Leverage (10-100x return), Neutral (1:1 return), or Overhead (return less than the effort) — treat them accordingly instead of giving every task equal polish. **When to use:** triaging your to-do list or your team's quarter; batch the L tasks into your best hours, do O tasks fast and sloppy. *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*
> "Pay attention to your fears because they're telling you something." — on why people procrastinate on their highest-leverage work

## Minimize opportunity cost, not ROI — Shreyas Doshi
In a high-leverage role, hundreds of tasks clear the bar of "positive ROI"; optimizing for ROI just pulls you toward quick wins because time sits in the denominator. **When to use:** quarterly planning — stop asking "is this a good use of my time," ask "is this the best use of my time." *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*
> "Is this the best use of my time?"

## Three levels of product work — Shreyas Doshi
Impact, execution, and optics are different altitudes of thinking; most cross-functional conflict is two smart people defaulting to different levels, not a real disagreement. **When to use:** diagnosing why a product review with execs keeps going sideways despite "rational" arguments. *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*

## Pre-mortem with tigers, paper tigers, elephants — Shreyas Doshi
Before launch, have the team imagine the project already failed and work backward: tigers are real threats, paper tigers are overrated ones, elephants are the thing nobody's saying out loud. **When to use:** any launch big enough that a bad surprise would be expensive to unwind. *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*
> "If you do a pre-mortem right, you will not have to do an ugly post-mortem."

## Most execution problems are strategy or culture problems — Shreyas Doshi
Constant firefighting and cross-team misalignment usually aren't solved by another sync meeting — they're a symptom of unclear strategy or a culture that punishes helping other teams. **When to use:** before proposing a new review cadence or process fix — check if a bandaid already fell off here once. *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*
> "Most execution problems... are actually not execution problems, they are either strategy problems or interpersonal problems or cultural problems."

## DHM model — Gibson Biddle
Strategy is a hypothesis that a bet will Delight customers, in a Hard-to-copy way, that also enHances Margin — all three have to be true or the bet isn't durable. **When to use:** stress-testing a feature idea before committing resources; kill it if it fails any leg. *Source: Gibson Biddle, "35 years of product design wisdom" (Netflix case studies)*
> "I'll bet my paycheck that within a week, Blockbuster's going to put a happy family on their couch, too" — on delight that isn't hard to copy

## JAM force-ranking — Gibson Biddle
Force-rank Growth, Engagement, and Monetization with your co-leaders and get explicit agreement on the order — most startup dysfunction is this triangle never being resolved out loud. **When to use:** when a CEO and CFO (or growth and product) keep clashing on what "good" means this quarter. *Source: Gibson Biddle, "35 years of product design wisdom"*

## Two-way door vs. one-way door — Gibson Biddle
Before agonizing over a decision, ask its magnitude (how much is really at stake relative to the business) and its reversibility — most decisions people treat as high-stakes are neither. **When to use:** any decision the team is over-deliberating; if it's small relative to the business and reversible, decide fast and move on. *Source: Gibson Biddle, "35 years of product design wisdom"*
> "Amazon calls those... a two-way door decision. It's reversible."

## Kill the two-percenters — Gibson Biddle
A feature that only 2% of users would touch adds permanent complexity for negligible reach — don't ship it just because it's clever or someone's pet idea. **When to use:** any time reach is being hand-waved in favor of a cute idea. *Source: Gibson Biddle, "35 years of product design wisdom"*
> "I don't like two percenters... we called it scraping the barnacles."

## Prioritization is the 5x skill — Ian McAllister
Given identical intelligence and resources, a PM with a genuinely strong prioritization instinct produces roughly five times the impact of one without it — it's the highest-leverage skill to build, above technical depth or design taste. **When to use:** deciding what a new PM should actually spend year one getting good at. *Source: Ian McAllister, "What it takes to become a top 1% PM"*
> "A product manager with a great innate ability to prioritize is going to generate 5X the impact of someone without that skill."

## Working backwards starts with the problem, not the parts bin — Ian McAllister
The tell that a team isn't really working backwards is when they pitch from capability ("we have these two technologies, we could combine them") instead of from a customer's problem. **When to use:** reviewing any PRD or press-release doc — if there's no compelling problem paragraph, there's no real problem. *Source: Ian McAllister, "What it takes to become a top 1% PM"*
> "There's different pieces in the pantry and we have these ingredients, we could put them together... that's not really working backwards."

## ICE + confidence meter — Itamar Gilad
Score ideas on Impact, Confidence, and Ease — but treat "confidence" as something you have to earn through evidence (fake-door test, data, experiment), not something you assign because you like the idea. **When to use:** any time a team's confidence scores all cluster suspiciously high. *Source: Itamar Gilad, "Becoming evidence-guided"*
> "Behind every terrible idea that was ever, someone thought it was great" — on why self-conviction is worth 0.01 out of 10 confidence

## Outcome roadmaps over release roadmaps — Itamar Gilad
A roadmap that commits to shipping a specific feature by a date kills learning before it starts; commit to an outcome by a date instead, and only lock the feature once you have evidence. **When to use:** replacing a Q3/Q4 feature-launch roadmap with one stated in outcomes. *Source: Itamar Gilad, "Becoming evidence-guided"*

## OKRs are a vitamin, not a medicine — Christina Wodtke
OKRs don't fix a broken org — they reveal what's already broken and amplify what's already working. Companies that "have their shit together" get supercharged; companies that don't get a faster, more visible failure. **When to use:** setting expectations before an OKR rollout. *Source: Christina Wodtke, "The ultimate guide to OKRs"*
> "I say OKRs are more of a vitamin, they're not a medicine."

## Set the goal so it's uncomfortable, not doomed — Christina Wodtke
Target roughly 70% attainment on key results; landing it exactly means you sandbagged, missing it badly means the goal was fantasy. **When to use:** calibrating ambition during quarterly OKR-setting. *Source: Christina Wodtke, "The ultimate guide to OKRs"*
> "A good goal is one that makes you feel somewhat uncomfortable but not doomed."

## Pilot with your best team, never your worst — Christina Wodtke
Give a new process (OKRs or otherwise) to the team most likely to make it work and adapt it to your culture, then propagate — piloting on a struggling team just makes everyone hate the process. **When to use:** rolling out any new process or framework; pick the strongest multidisciplinary team as the pilot and check in after a quarter. *Source: Christina Wodtke, "The ultimate guide to OKRs"*
> "If you try to fix a bad team with OKRs, you'll make everybody hate OKRs and make the bad team worse."

## No more than one step away from the company goal — Matt LeMay
A team's goal should connect to the company's top-line goal in one obvious step ("if we hit this, revenue moves by this much") — not through five layers of cascaded OKRs nobody can trace back. **When to use:** setting team-level goals; if you can't draw the one-step line, the goal is wrong. *Source: Matt LeMay, "The one question that saves product careers"*
> "How does this all add up to the company-level growth goals?"

## The Low-Impact PM Death Spiral — Matt LeMay
Teams default to low-risk cosmetic work (rhinestones on the car) because it invites less scrutiny than touching the engine; the more of that a product accumulates, the harder high-impact work becomes, which pushes teams toward more low-impact work. **When to use:** naming why a product feels bloated and unshippable despite constant activity. *Source: Matt LeMay, "The one question that saves product careers"*
> "Low-impact work begets low-impact work."

## Never say yes or no — surface the trade-off — Matt LeMay
When a stakeholder with formal authority demands something that looks low-impact, don't fight it head-on and don't cave silently — lay out the goal, the alternatives, and the trade-offs, and let them decide with full information. **When to use:** pushback from an exec or founder on a request you disagree with. *Source: Matt LeMay, "The one question that saves product careers"*
> "If you're doing product management really well, you never have to say yes and you never have to say no. You're giving people options and you're helping them understand the trade-offs."

## Hesitation is the costliest decision — Ben Horowitz
The worst failure mode for a leader isn't picking the wrong option between two bad choices — it's stalling on the decision entirely, which signals dysfunction to the whole org and invites others to grab power in the vacuum. **When to use:** any decision where every option looks bad; pick the least-bad one and move. *Source: Ben Horowitz, "$46B of hard truths"*
> "The worst thing that you do as a leader is you hesitate on the next decision."
> "If everybody agrees with the decision, then you didn't add any value because they would've done that without you."
