# Product & Business Strategy: Distilled Wisdom

> distilled: 2026-08-20, corpus @ be8ab89

## Outcomes over output — Marty Cagan
A feature team ships a roadmap of features and calls it done; an empowered product team is given a problem and only gets credit if it actually solves it. **When to use:** any time a roadmap is a list of deliverables instead of a list of problems — that's the tell you're a feature team. *Source: Marty Cagan, "Product management theater"*
> "It is a lot easier to deliver output than it is to deliver outcomes."
> "The harder part is time to money and I know that's what they care about."

## PM as creator, not facilitator — Marty Cagan
If your job description is "I ask why" and "I facilitate," you're a project manager wearing a PM title, and you're dramatically overpaid for the value you provide. **When to use:** diagnosing whether a PM role (or your own role) is real product management or administrative theater. *Source: Marty Cagan, "Product management theater"*
> "A product manager is a creator, not a facilitator."

## DHM: Delight, Hard-to-copy, Margin-enhancing — Gibson Biddle
Good product strategy is a hypothesis that a bet will delight customers in ways competitors can't easily copy and that ultimately makes money — not just "customers like it." **When to use:** stress-testing a roadmap bet before funding it — ask what stops a competitor from shipping the same thing next week. *Source: Gibson Biddle, "35 years of product design wisdom"*
> "I'll bet my paycheck that within a week, Blockbuster's going to put a happy family on their couch, too."

## LNO framework — Shreyas Doshi
Not all tasks deserve equal effort: L(everage) tasks return 10-100x on the effort you put in, N(eutral) tasks return roughly what you put in, O(verhead) tasks return less than you put in — the same activity (a bug report, a set of notes) can be any of the three depending on context. **When to use:** when you're stretched thin and defaulting to perfectionism on everything; triage your to-do list into L/N/O before deciding where to spend polish. *Source: Shreyas Doshi, "The art of product management"*
> "Many people who are ambitious or are perfectionists by default treat each of these types of tasks the same way, and therein lies the problem."

## Opportunity Solution Tree — Teresa Torres
Map from a single outcome down through customer opportunities to candidate solutions and experiments, so discovery has structure instead of teams jumping straight to a solution. **When to use:** when a team says "we're doing discovery" but their opportunity space is already full of solutions in disguise. *Source: Teresa Torres, "Build better products with continuous product discovery"*
> "I define continuous discovery as building in those continuous feedback loops."

## PMF decays by default — Casey Winters
Product-market fit is not a state you reach and keep; user expectations and the competitive bar rise continuously, so a product that fits the market today quietly falls out of fit if no one is pushing it forward. **When to use:** as a standing falsifiable check — if your usage/retention curve is flat while competitors ship, you're losing fit even without a single visible failure. *Source: Casey Winters, "Why most product managers are unprepared for the demands of a real startup"*
> "You might find yourself fall out of product market fit entirely... it's going to take a long time to figure that out."

## Scalable acquisition is part of PMF, not a step after it — Casey Winters
A product that retains well but that you can't grow doesn't count as having product-market fit — the growth loop is now considered a requirement, not a post-PMF luxury. **When to use:** evaluating "we have PMF" claims — ask whether there's a repeatable way to get new users, not just whether existing ones stick. *Source: Casey Winters, "Why most product managers are unprepared for the demands of a real startup"*
> "If you got a product that retains well and you can't find more users for it, I don't think that's product market fit."

## Differentiated value, not features — April Dunford
Positioning starts by naming the real competitive alternative (often the status quo, a spreadsheet, or "do nothing") and then isolating the value only you deliver against that alternative — not a features list. **When to use:** before writing any pitch or landing page copy; force the team to answer "what would the customer do if we didn't exist?" first. *Source: April Dunford, "A step-by-step guide to crafting a sales pitch that wins"*
> "Differentiated value is the answer to the question why pick us over the other alternatives?"

## A good pivot goes warmer, not colder — Dalton Caldwell
Successful pivots move toward founder expertise and prior learning, not away from it — Brex went from Brazilian fintech to corporate cards, Retool built on dashboards they'd already built internally. **When to use:** evaluating a proposed pivot — if it moves you further from what you're already an expert in, be suspicious. *Source: Dalton Caldwell, "Lessons from 1,000+ YC startups"*
> "A good pivot is like going home. It's warmer, it's closer to something that you're an expert at."

## Tar-pit ideas — Dalton Caldwell
Some categories of startup idea (Foursquare clones, marketplaces-for-X) are perpetually appealing, generate enough positive feedback to feel validating, and reliably fail — the validation itself is what traps founders. **When to use:** when an idea keeps attracting easy enthusiasm from friends/investors but no paying traction — treat the ease of validation as a warning sign, not a green light. *Source: Dalton Caldwell, "Lessons from 1,000+ YC startups"*
> "That's why it's a tarpit, is it draws you in and you get stuck because it seems like it's a good idea and you get all this positive feedback."

## When to actually pivot — Dalton Caldwell
Look at how many untried growth ideas remain, not how the metrics look this week — if you're out of ideas, pivot; if there are a dozen you haven't tried yet, run them first. **When to use:** as a falsifiable gate before declaring an idea dead — count the untested ideas, don't just eyeball morale. *Source: Dalton Caldwell, "Lessons from 1,000+ YC startups"*
> "I would look at how many more ideas the founder has on how to make it grow. If it's not going well and you're out of ideas, that is usually a good time to pivot."

## Don't adopt SAFe by default — Melissa Perri
Scaled Agile Framework is popular with executives precisely because it hands them a plug-and-play map, but practitioners who succeed with it consistently end up ripping it apart and rebuilding something custom. **When to use:** when leadership proposes SAFe (or any heavyweight scaling framework) as the fix for coordination problems — treat it as a default to argue against, not toward. *Source: Melissa Perri, "Everything you've ever wanted to know about SAFe and the product owner role"*
> "I do not recommend using SAFe. Every single person I have talked to who likes SAFe, found success with SAFe, they ended up ripping it up and making it into something else."

## Frameworks are a symptom, not a cure — Melissa Perri
Teams that already talk to each other and break down work together don't need Scrum, stand-ups, or any named process — frameworks exist to compensate for orgs that never grew up building software, not to make good teams better. **When to use:** before installing process on a team — ask whether the underlying communication problem exists at all. *Source: Melissa Perri, "Everything you've ever wanted to know about SAFe and the product owner role"*
> "If you're not doing that, that's where I think things like a framework help, but if you already are doing that, you don't need a framework."

## Positioning starts with the alternative, not the product — April Dunford
Before you can articulate value, you have to pin down what the customer would actually do if you didn't exist — often not a competitor at all, but a spreadsheet, an intern, or inertia. **When to use:** any time positioning work starts with "here's what makes us different" instead of "here's what you'd do instead of us." *Source: April Dunford, "A step-by-step guide to crafting a sales pitch that wins"*
> "If we didn't exist in the market, what would a customer do?"
